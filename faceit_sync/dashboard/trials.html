Trialists

Local tool — gitignored, never published. Click a column to sort, click a player to expand their maps and modes. Win rate is 82% explained by which team someone was on (measured across 1016 players), so read it next to the team column; Eff is only 22%, which makes it the fairest single number here. Elo and Eff are relative to a player’s own region and tier — a Master figure and an Advanced one are not the same unit.

Methodology

What every column means, where it comes from, and what it cannot tell you. The thresholds below are written in from the code at page build, so this section cannot drift from the maths it describes.

Where the numbers come from

Built by faceit-sync trials from the local FACEIT database, through the same payload builder that produces owdb.io, and computed by the same code — pure.js is inlined here unchanged. A number on this page and the same number on the site cannot disagree. Every division in the database is included, not only the current season: judging a trialist wants every map on record. Nothing here is published.

Finding a player

Search matches the FACEIT nickname or the in-game name, case-insensitive substring, across every region at once. Those two names often differ, and a recruiting shortlist is normally written in the in-game one — of a real 24-name sheet, 3 resolved by nickname and 13 by in-game name. A player who appears in several divisions collapses to one result; a name that matches nothing usually means they do not play FACEIT League at all.

Maps

Counted from the per-game rosters of the regular season and the playoffs. The team roster's own game count is deliberately ignored: it is rolled up per championship and playoffs are a separate championship, so it stops at the group stage and undercounts anyone whose team went deep.

Win %, team %, and vs team

Win % is the player's own map record. Read it next to team %, which is their team's record over its whole season, including maps the player sat out. Measured across 1016 players, a player's win rate is 82% explained by which team they were on; it is closer to a team statistic than a personal one.

vs team is the difference. It is structurally zero for anyone who started every map — they are the team, so there is nothing to diverge from; those cells are dimmed rather than shown as a bold zero. It carries real signal only for substitutes and mid-season arrivals, which is exactly the trialist case.

Eff

Each player's per-map averages — damage, healing, mitigation, K/D — expressed as a z-score against the same role in the same division, then averaged. +1.00 means one standard deviation above that peer group. Needs …+ maps and …+ peers, and shows an em dash otherwise. The peer group is named beside every value, because it is the unit: only 22% of Eff is explained by team quality against 82% for win rate, which makes it the fairest single number here.

Eff·n — and why it, not Eff, sets the order

Eff averages z-scores of per-map figures, so a short season is not merely less reliable, it is wider. Across 1123 players the spread of Eff is 0.679 at 5–14 maps against 0.356 at 50+, and 12.6% of low-sample players post an Eff beyond ±1 against 1.0% of high-sample ones. The averages themselves barely move — the correlation between maps and Eff is +0.165, faintly upward — so this is spread, not bias. Sorting on raw Eff therefore fills the top of the list with whoever played least.

Eff·n is Eff pulled toward the group mean in proportion to sample: Eff × n/(n+…), the standard regression-to-the-mean correction. It only ever scales toward zero — a sign never flips, and an Eff that was below its floor stays absent rather than becoming a number. Raw Eff keeps its own column; only the ranking changes. Its own limit: it weights the player's sample, not the size of the cohort they were scored against, so an Eff computed against the minimum peers is thin in a way this does not catch — check the peer count printed beside Eff.

Elo

FACEIT's matchmaking rating, shown and sortable. It feeds no calculation on this page — not Eff, not any rate or ranking. That is enforced by a test which recomputes every number with all elo stripped out and fails if anything moves. Treat it as context only: it is tier-relative and reflects solo-queue history as much as league play.

Roles, and the “2nd role” tag

A player sits in the table of the role they played most, from FACEIT's per-game role. A second role played on at least …% of their maps earns them a row in that table too, tagged 2nd role. In that second table their stats and Eff are marked all roles: the data carries no per-role split, so a flex player's tank row shows season-wide averages and a peer group from their main role. The numbers are shown because they are the only ones that exist, not because they are role-specific.

Hitscan and flex DPS are assigned by hand

The buttons on any damage player file them into a Hitscan or Flex DPS table; anyone unassigned stays under plain Damage. It is manual because it cannot honestly be inferred — telling the two apart needs hero attribution, which exists for 128 players in the whole dataset. Your choices are remembered per browser and outlive the pool, so dropping someone and adding them back later does not lose the call you made.

This is a grouping, not a recalculation. Splitting the table does not change anyone's Eff or its peer group: Eff is z-scored against a division-wide cohort, and labels covering only the handful of players in your pool cannot form one. The peer group printed beside each Eff is still the real one — which is sometimes already a seat, where captures placed that player in one. A seat also only ever replaces the Damage table: a flex player keeps the tank table they earned on their own maps.

Per-mode and per-map records

Inside an expanded row, with the team's rate for the same mode or map beside each one. A mode needs …+ games and a map …+; below that the rate is an em dash rather than a number, because a record over two or three maps is not a rate.

Hero pool

The one genuinely thin section. Heroes come from replay captures, not the FACEIT feed: 122 games and 128 players in the whole dataset, so most players have none at all. A win rate needs …+ captured games on that hero. An empty pool means nobody captured their games — never that they play few heroes, and a pool showing one hero at "100%" is usually one captured match, not a one-trick.

Tier and region are not a common scale

Eff is z-scored inside one division and elo is region-and-tier relative, so +0.9 in Advanced is not +0.9 in Master. Every row names its region and tier. No cross-tier normalisation is attempted, because there is no defensible one — comparing across tiers is a judgement call the tool leaves to you.

What this deliberately does not do

  • Blizzard / competitive rank. There are no BattleTags in the data — not one of 1347 players — so there is nothing to join on, and Blizzard publishes no stats API.
  • Strength-of-schedule adjustment. Measured and rejected: mean opponent win rate spans only 47–57% within a division while team quality spans 35–96%. In a round robin everyone plays everyone, so adjusting for it adds noise.
  • Team-adjusted Eff (Eff minus the player's own team mean). It removes the residual 22% but asks “better than your teammates?” rather than “how good?”, which punishes a strong player on a strong team.
  • Any single composite score. One number hides which axis a player is actually good at, which is the whole question when comparing trialists.
